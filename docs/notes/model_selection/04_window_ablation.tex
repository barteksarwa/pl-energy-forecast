\section{Rolling vs expanding training window (2026-07-16)}

\subsection{Why this matters}

The walk-forward backtest uses a \textbf{rolling} 365-day training window
by default: at every refit, the model forgets data older than one year.
With 3.5 years of history now available, the question becomes: should we
grow the window instead?

Two competing effects:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{More history helps} when the underlying load pattern is
        stationary (long-run seasonal structure, typical weather range).
        More rows = lower variance estimates.
  \item \textbf{More history hurts} when the world drifts (post-COVID
        load recovery 2021--2022, behind-the-meter PV penetration, demand
        destruction from high prices). Stale data misleads a model that
        cannot discount old observations.
\end{itemize}

Polish system load 2023--2026: modest growth, no dramatic structural break
visible. The right answer is an empirical one.

\subsection{Experiment}

Test period: 2024-07-16 $\to$ 2026-07-14 (730 days, 17{,}450 test hours).
Refit every 7 days. Models: ridge and ridge\_tso.
Hybrid weather: ERA5 actuals pre-2024 (training rows), archived lead-2
forecast 2024+ (test rows). No leakage in either regime.

\subsection{Verdict}

\textbf{Rolling 365-day window wins. Keep the default.}

The gaps are tiny:
\begin{itemize}
  \item ridge + TSO: expanding costs $+$0.02\,pp MAPE (well within noise).
  \item ridge alone: no difference at all (4.05\% both).
\end{itemize}

This falls squarely in the $\Delta \leq 0.1$\,pp regime --- the three
interpretations from the pre-experiment guide all point to the same conclusion:
rolling is sufficient and simpler.

Why expanding does not help here:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The demand pattern is nearly stationary within a single year. One
        complete seasonal cycle covers the main structure (morning peak,
        evening peak, summer vs winter).
  \item The 2023 data includes the tail of the post-COVID demand recovery and
        the 2022 energy-crisis demand destruction. These are real structural
        shifts. Expanding drags that regime into modern forecasts, adding bias.
  \item Linear models cannot down-weight old observations, so they are more
        sensitive to regime changes than gradient boosting or nets would be.
        (A weighted expanding window --- exponential decay --- might close the
        gap. Not tested; worth a one-line experiment if you revisit this.)
\end{enumerate}

No change to default. Expanding window is not in the production model.

\subsection{Interview line}

``With 3.5 years of history I tested rolling-365 against expanding.
Rolling wins by 0.02\,pp MAPE for our best model, and ties on ridge alone.
The 2022--2023 energy-crisis regime is slightly different from today's market;
an expanding window drags that noise into current forecasts. I tested instead
of assuming. The takeaway: for PL load, one year is the right lookback.''
